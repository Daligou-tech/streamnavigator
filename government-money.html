What it refuses to do

These four rules are the reason to trust the rest of it. Each one is a way a confident-sounding report leaves a household worse off, and each is enforced by code that can only ever remove a line from your shortlist — never add one. The test enumerates every combination of answers you can give and checks that not one escapes.

D1A credit against tax you do not oweMost of the large residential credits reduce what you owe rather than paying out. In a year you owe nothing they are worth nothing — and that is the case where the obvious answer is most wrong, because a household that has just spent thousands on a heat pump is exactly the one most pleased to hear about a credit. If you tell us you expect no federal tax, no non-refundable credit reaches your shortlist.
D3One annual ceiling, counted twiceSeveral kinds of efficiency work share a single yearly cap. Three jobs in one year is one ceiling, not three. Where two of your lines share one, both say so — and if more work is planned, the report tells you the date after which it would count against next year’s ceiling instead.
D4A rebate and a credit, added togetherA utility rebate usually reduces the cost a federal credit is calculated on, so the two do not add up. Where both touch the same purchase, both lines carry the order to work them out in.
D5A discovery your installer already madeWhoever sold you the solar, the EV or the heat pump almost certainly mentioned the credit at the time. Those come back as “confirm you already claimed this”, because telling you what you already know is not worth $39.

And the thing it holds no opinion about at all

  • How much you will get. The catalogue behind this report holds who each program is for, what it is gated on, which programs it interacts with, and the office that administers it. It holds no dollar amounts, no percentages, no caps, no income thresholds and no deadlines — on purpose, because those are exactly the facts that go stale silently, and a figure quoted from a file written last September is no better than one half-remembered. Every line tells you what governs the amount and where to get today’s version of it.

What this does not do

Read this before you start. It is most of what separates this from a confident-sounding guess.

  • We do not hold a live program database and we do not look programs up. The catalogue holds gates and authorities, was last reviewed on the date printed in your report, and holds no amounts at all. Every line names the office that can confirm the current figure, the current rules and the current deadline.
  • We do not tell you what you will get. Most of these figures are ceilings worked out from what you actually spent, several reduce tax owed rather than paying out, and rebates often change what a credit is calculated on. We tell you which programs apply and what governs the amount. You confirm the number.
  • We do not ask for a login, a bank connection, a tax return or a Social Security number. There is no option to give us any of them, and the free scorecard never leaves your browser at all.
  • We do not file anything for you and we never contact an agency or a utility on your behalf. You make every claim yourself, or your tax preparer does.
  • We cannot know whether a program still has funding. Rebate pots close when they empty and reopen on a new fiscal year, and nothing here can see that. Where it matters the line says to ask before you spend.
  • Utility rebates are not government money, and we include them anyway because they sit on the same purchases, change what the federal credits are worth, and are missed just as often. Every one is marked as a utility line so you know who you are dealing with.
  • We cannot make the work go away. A shortlist of six programs is an evening of phone calls and forms. The report puts them in order and tells you who to call first, but you make the calls.

Questions

Why can’t I just search for this myself?
You can, and for one specific program you probably should. What takes the time is the other direction — working out which of dozens of programs are even worth reading about, given that you rent, or that you will owe no federal tax this year, or that you have already used this year’s ceiling, or that your state has no income tax for a credit to sit on. That narrowing is what you are buying, and the report shows its working: every program ruled out, with the fact that ruled it out. The confirming you still do yourself, and every line tells you where.
Will you tell me exactly how much I’ll get?
No, and anyone who does is guessing. Most of these figures are ceilings computed from what you actually spent; several are credits against tax owed, which are worth nothing to a household that owes nothing; and a utility rebate often reduces the amount a federal credit is calculated on. The catalogue behind this report holds no figures at all, on purpose — so the report cannot state one even by accident. It tells you which programs apply, what governs the amount, and where to compute it.
How current is this?
The catalogue is dated, and your report prints the date it was last reviewed. What it holds is the durable part — who a program is for, what it is gated on, which programs interact, and who administers it — which changes on a timescale of years. Amounts, caps, thresholds and deadlines change every year, so we hold none of them and send you to the authority instead. If the catalogue is ever more than eighteen months old, every line in your report is automatically downgraded to “worth checking” and says why.
Do you file the claims for me?
No — you get the line, the reasoning and the office to take it to, but you, or your tax preparer, submit the actual claim. We never contact an agency or a utility on your behalf.
Why do you insist on knowing my state?
Because federal programs are the same everywhere and everything else is not. It is also one of the few genuinely decisive facts we hold: eight states have no broad individual income tax, so for a household in one of them a state income-tax credit is ruled out outright rather than hedged. A ZIP code helps too — it tells us which utility territory you are in.
What if I don’t qualify for anything?
You will know before you pay — the scorecard shows it free. What the report gives you then is all twenty-eight programs named and the specific fact that rules each one out. And if nothing at all comes back — no shortlist and nothing even worth confirming — the charge is refunded automatically, you keep the report, and you do not have to ask: the engine knows the shortlist was empty, so the refund is queued the moment the report is written. If what does come back turns out not to be worth acting on, that is a judgement call rather than something we can detect, so reply to the delivery email and we refund it in full.
What if I come back next year?
Each report is its own $39 and there is no subscription. But if you buy a second one under the same email, it is compared against your first: the report leads with what opened since then — you bought the house, the work got finished, the household changed — and what closed. That comparison is the only honest basis for telling somebody to look at something again, and a cold report can never make it.
Does this cover anything besides energy?
Yes — property tax relief, first-home assistance, childcare, education, retirement saving, health insurance premium help, and assistance programs gated on income and household size. Energy is simply where the most money has been recently, which is why it comes up most.
